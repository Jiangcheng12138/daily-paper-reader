Abstract 23 

Neural circuits produce reliable activity even after environmental disturbances. This occurs because 24 

neurons respond to perturbations in a compensatory manner, a process termed homeostatic 25 

plasticity. Bullfrogs undergo prolonged periods underwater during winter, when lung ventilation and 26 

its neural control system ceases activity, but air-breathing resumes unscathed when environmental 27 

temperatures increase weeks to months later. Compensatory neural mechanisms that contribute 28 

involve upregulation of excitatory synaptic transmission on motoneurons driven by inactivity, but 29 

whether inactivity or acclimation to low temperatures drive other forms of compensation is not 30 

known. The GABA A receptor contribution to respiratory rhythm generation is downregulated following 31 

overwintering, which promotes network excitability. Therefore, we disentangled the contributions of 32 

cold temperature acclimation and inactivity experienced during overwintering on reduced GABAergic 33 

signaling. Here, we show that cold temperature, and not inactivity, reduces GABA A signaling in the 34 

respiratory rhythm generating network, without influencing GABA A transmission onto motoneurons. 35 

Therefore, cold temperature acclimation drives reduced GABAergic signaling selectively in inter-36 

neuronal rhythm generating circuits, while excitatory motoneurons synapses are strengthened by 37 

inactivity in the overwintering environment. Most work interprets compensatory plasticity as activity-38 

dependent during activity perturbations, but we reveal that different aspects of a disruptive 39 

environment elicit distinct forms of plasticity across a motor network. 40

Introduction 44 

Changing environmental conditions ( e.g. temperature, acidity, salinity, oxygen availability) tend 45 

to destabilize the organism’s internal environment and therefore, its function. It is widely recognized 46 

that organisms use various sets of feedback mechanisms throughout the body that compensate for 47 

internal variation to maintain physiological function ( i.e. homeostasis). Neural circuits are thought to 48 

sense changes and regulate activity during perturbations by adjustments in synaptic strength and 49 

intrinsic membrane properties. This elicits compensatory changes in excitation or inhibition that aim 50 

to maintain relatively stable neural physiological function, termed ‘homeostatic plasticity’ [1,2]. While 51 

most studies indicate that homeostatic regulation during activity perturbations are dependent on 52 

activity sensing and transduction [1,3–5], concomitant abiotic environmental conditions may also 53 

affect network activity. For example, during winter cold temperatures may induce hibernation where 54 

body temperature and energy requirements are reduced for prolonged periods [6]. Consequently, low 55 

temperatures reduce metabolism, and therefore the demands for many neuronal functions, which 56 

could each in principle act as signals for plasticity. Therefore, the degree to which reduced neural 57 

activity vs. factors associated with a complex environment that ultimately reduce neural activity in the 58 

first place serve as signal for compensation can be obscure. 59 

An extreme example of this scenario occurs in the respiratory network of American bullfrogs 60 

and other ranid frogs that inhabit northern latitudes. Lung ventilation is necessary for metabolic 61 

homeostasis; however, at cold temperatures, gas exchange requirements can be fully provided by 62 

cutaneous respiration, allowing frogs to stay underwater for long periods without requiring air-63 

breathing [7]. Therefore, during hibernation, the respiratory circuits responsible for producing lung 64 

ventilation are silent [8]. Consistent with homeostatic theory [9,10], inactivity serves as a key signal 65 

that enhances excitability during hibernation by upregulating excitatory AMPA-glutamate receptors on 66 

motoneurons [11–13]. Interestingly, the enhancement of AMPA receptors on motoneurons correlated 67 

with greater evoked synaptic transmission selectively at cool temperatures, indicating that 68 

homeostatic regulation can have broader effects on network robustness than globally enhancing 69 

excitability [13]. Therefore, in a complex environment with multiple interacting variables, inactivity 70

While homeostatic tuning rules upregulate excitatory synaptic receptors, it is not clear if these 73 

same processes also control other forms of compensation that arise during hibernation. For example, 74 

inhibitory signaling in neural circuits are mainly orchestrated by ligand-gated Cl - channels activated by 75 

the neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) through GABA A receptors [14]. After overwintering, 76 

the respiratory network has a reduced contribution of GABA A receptors to rhythm generation. In this 77 

way, the application of GABA A receptor antagonist typically stops respiratory-related activity in 78 

controls, while it had no effect after hibernation. The loss of GABA A signaling in hibernators had the 79 

unexpected consequence of enhancing excitability at cool temperatures by removing inhibition that is 80 

stimulated during cooling to slow activity in controls [15], a surprising parallel to enhanced 81 

transmission at excitatory motoneuron synapses due to upregulated receptors through homeostatic 82 

mechanisms [13]. These results suggest that both AMPA receptors and the GABA system may be 83 

modulated by inactivity during hibernation. However, it remains possible that GABA may be controlled 84 

independently. In cultured cortical neurons, excitatory synapses and intrinsic excitability were 85 

enhanced during inactivity by mechanisms involving calcium/calmodulin kinase, while GABA inhibition 86 

was decreased via separate and undetermined mechanisms [16]. In addition, circulating levels of 87 

GABA in warm-acclimated epaulette sharks ( Hemiscyllium ocellatum ) are comparatively higher than 88 

cold-acclimated sharks [17], indicating that temperature per se may play a role in the regulation of the 89 

GABA system. Therefore, we investigated what stimulus (i.e. cold-acclimation or inactivity during 90 

overwintering conditions) reduces GABA A signaling in the central pattern generator of the bullfrogs. 91 

We also examined whether those stimuli affect GABA A signaling on motoneurons using whole cell 92 

patch-clamp to measure evoked inhibitory postsynaptic currents (IPSC). This allowed us to 93 

discriminate the location of GABAergic compensatory plasticity within an ecologically meaningful 94 

context of the overwintering environment. 95

Adult American bullfrogs ( Aquarana catesbeiana ) were acquired from Niles Biological 99 

(Sacramento, CA, USA) and maintained in the facilities from the Division of Biological Sciences of the 100 

University of Missouri (Columbia, MO). Frogs were randomly assigned to one of three experimental 101 

groups which were kept in plastic tanks with dechlorinated water continuously bubbled with room air, 102 

and a 12:12h light:dark cycle. The control group was kept at room temperature (~22°C) and fed once a 103 

week with pellets provided by the seller. Two groups of cold-acclimated frogs (ventilating and 104 

overwintered) were kept at a temperature-controlled room set to 20°C for approximately 3 days 105 

whereafter room temperatures were reduced by 2°C per day until it reached 4°C. One group of cold-106 

acclimated frogs were kept with a layer of water covering half of their body, thus allowing lung 107 

ventilation, whereas for the overwintering group plastic nets were placed in the water level as soon as 108 

room temperature reached 4°C, which impeded lung ventilation. Buccal pumping in the ventilating 109 

group was visually confirmed upon daily animal welfare checks. Cold-acclimated frogs from both 110 

ventilating and overwintered groups were maintained at least 2 weeks under 4°C before 111 

commencement of experiments, which was sufficient to increase the amplitude of spontaneous 112 

excitatory postsynaptic currents in this species [12]. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care 113 

and Use Committee from the University of Missouri (protocols #39264 and #65623). 114

Electrophysiological protocol 130 

For the electrophysiology experiments, after dissection the brain-spinal cord was glued to an 131 

agar block and sliced (300 μm thick) with a vibrating microtome (Technical Products International 132 

Series 1000, St. Louis, MO, USA). Voltage clamp experiments were conducted using the same 133 

equipment previously described [12]. Thin walled glass pipettes (resistance: 3-8 MΩ) were fabricated 134 

using a Sutter Instruments puller (model P87, Novato, CA, USA) and filled with an intracellular solution 135

solution of aCSF containing the glycine receptor antagonist, strychnine (5 μM), and the ionotropic 138

isolated GABA A currents, we validated this protocol by adding the GABA A receptor antagonist, 140

monitored with a thermocouple placed close to the tissue. 147

Data analysis and statistics 163 

Bursting frequency (bursts/min) from nerve rootlets was assessed by counting the amount of 164 

respiratory bursts during 5 min. We counted respiratory bursts just before applying bicuculline to the 165 

bath, and in the last 5 min of an exposure of 30 min long (Fig. 1). IPSCmax (-pA) were the averaged 166 

amplitude of three maximum evoked currents. IPSCmin (-pA) were the average of the amplitude of 167 

the evoked minimum currents identified with the ‘Peak Analysis’ module from LabChart (v.8.0, 168 

ADInstruments). Release probability (%) measures the probability of release of neurotransmitters and 169 

was calculated as the percentage of events that evoked an IPSCmin during the five minutes of 170 

stimulation. 171 

Differences between groups were tested with a one-way ANOVA followed by a Holm-Šydák 172 

post hoc test. When data did not comprise with the assumption of normality, differences were tested 173 

with a Kruskal-Wallis followed by a Dunn test for pairwise comparisons. All statistics were performed 174 

with R v.4.6.0 [22–24]. Data are mean ± SD. 175

Discussion 199 

Hibernation reduces GABA A signaling in the bullfrog respiratory network [15]. The inhibition of 200 

respiratory bursts by bicuculline in A. catesbeiana under control conditions, and the maintenance of 201 

respiratory bursts in overwintered frogs was previously described [15,21]. Contrary to our 202 

expectations based on excitatory motoneuron synapses [13], this reduction in GABA A signaling was 203 

localized to the rhythm generating circuits and entirely driven by cold-acclimation, rather than 204 

inactivity of the motor system that occurs during hibernation. While both forms of plasticity enhance 205 

function of the network at cool temperatures [13,15], these results demonstrate two apparently 206 

‘homeostatic’ network modifications (i.e., those that oppose an activity perturbation) may be 207 

controlled by entirely distinct aspects of a complex environment. 208 

The present results support that cold-acclimation, but not inactivity, reduces the reliance on 209 

the GABA A system in the bullfrog respiratory network. We make this conclusion because bicuculline 210

the ground squirrel ( Citellus citellus ), GABA levels increased in the pons and spinal cord when 214 

temperature was experimentally shifted from 5°C to 35°C, initiating the process of arousal from the 215 

hibernating state within 1.5-2.5 h [26]. At high concentrations, GABA may desensitize GABA A216 

receptors, ultimately reducing membrane conductance to Cl - [14,27–29]. During our experiments, 217 

bullfrogs acclimated to 4°C were dissected and later tested at room temperature of approximately 218 

22°C, which may have elevated the GABA concentration and consequent desensitization of GABA A219 

receptors. On the other hand, the brainstem of 13-lined ground squirrel ( Ictidomys tridecemlineatus )220 

undergoes seasonal changes in the expression of GABA A receptor subunit composition, which suggests 221 

GABA A receptor expression may vary according to environmental temperatures [30]. While the 222 

mechanistic relationship between cold-acclimation and the GABA system (e.g., whether receptors are 223 

downregulated or presynaptic GABA synthesis is reduced) remains to be explored, our results 224 

nevertheless are consistent with the idea that inactivity does not drive these responses. 225 

Synaptic inhibition of premotor neurons are governed by GABA A and glycine receptors coupled 226 

with Cl - ionotropic channels, and are pivotal for function of rhythmic oscillatory networks [31–35]. 227 

Contrary to mammals, where blockade of GABA A receptors does not stop respiratory networks [36], 228 

the current data, in accordance with previous observations [15,21,37] unequivocally demonstrate that 229 

GABA A receptors are crucial in generating ventilatory rhythm for lung ventilation in adult amphibians. 230 

Blockade of glycine receptors also silences respiratory bursts in A. catesbeiana [37]. Since respiratory 231 

network activity persists during the application of bicuculline in both ventilators and overwintered 232 

bullfrogs in the present study, it is possible that cold-acclimation somehow causes glycine receptors to 233 

play a more dominant role in generating the respiratory rhythm. Alternatively, pacemaker neurons 234 

dependent on persistent Na + currents or Ca 2+ -activated nonspecific cationic currents were 235 

hypothesized as a mechanism accounting for rhythmogenesis [32,33,38,39]. In A. catesbeiana , it was 236 

suggested that lung-related bursts of tadpoles switch their rhythmogenic mechanism from 237 

pacemaking to network inhibition when they metamorphose to adults [40]. Likewise, the perspective 238

Conclusions 242 

The present results demonstrate that during hibernation, cold-acclimation is responsible for 243 

the changes regarding inhibitory GABAergic signaling in the respiratory network of the American 244 

bullfrog, A. catesbeiana . Additionally, inhibitory synapses in the hypoglossal motoneuron are 245 

unaffected during overwintering, which is in stark contrast to AMPA-glutamate excitatory synapses 246 

where inactivity during overwintering is the main factor leading to synaptic plasticity [13]. Therefore, 247 

excitatory and inhibitory synapses in different parts of the network respond to different components 248 

of the same environmental stressors during hibernation. This is in accordance with previous 249 

observations suggesting independent regulatory mechanisms between excitatory and inhibitory 250 

synaptic scaling [16]. These results highlight that the plethora of environmental inputs received by an 251 

organism elicit various and complex mechanisms driving homeostatic responses in an ecologically 252 

realistic context. 253
